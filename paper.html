Decision-Space Collapse in Advisory Language Models

Measuring trajectory omission, framing sensitivity, and recovery through Decision-Space Integrity.

Andrew J. Cousins

Revision notice — September 2026

This paper reports a historical experiment using the Decision-Space Integrity measurement system available at the time of the study. Subsequent research has identified important limitations in measurement identity, evaluator traceability and comparability between instrument versions. The original run data have been preserved, but the exact historical scorer implementation cannot be bound to a specific source commit.

The results reported here therefore remain the results of the original study and have not been retrospectively rescored. A methods-focused publication addressing measurement identity explicitly has since been published: The Ruler Is Part of the Result (DOI 10.5281/zenodo.22919868). Any future reanalysis of the historical corpus will be reported as a new study rather than as a silent revision of these results.

Abstract summary

This preprint introduces decision-space collapse: a failure mode in which advisory language model outputs may make some plausible decision paths visible while leaving others out. Decision-Space Integrity (DSI) is presented as a measurement framework for auditing configured expected-path visibility, trajectory omission, framing sensitivity, and recovery.

Claim boundary

This work measures visibility of configured expected paths in model outputs. It does not measure advice quality, factual correctness, user outcomes, or regulatory compliance.

Author

Who is behind this work?

Andrew J. Cousins

Andrew J. Cousins is an enterprise systems engineer and independent AI researcher working on AI measurement, assurance and governance. His research operationalises Decision-Space Collapse as a measurable failure mode in which an AI response can omit configured decision paths while remaining otherwise useful or locally acceptable. He created Decision-Space Integrity (DSI), a measurement and assurance architecture for comparing governed expected structure with what an AI output makes visible, while preserving measurement identity and comparability over time. His subsequent work has examined reproducibility, measurement authority, comparability and governed intervention, with an emphasis on preserving human decision-making agency and making AI evaluation more inspectable.
